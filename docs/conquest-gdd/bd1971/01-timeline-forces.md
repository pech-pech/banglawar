# Bangladesh Liberation War 1971: Timeline, Forces and Organisation

Research notes for a strategy-game theme played from the Bangladeshi side. Tone is neutral and factual. The war involved mass civilian deaths, displacement and sexual violence; game text should treat this with care. Every fact line ends with a bracketed source number that refers to the Sources list. Where a number appears in a tertiary source (Wikipedia) it is marked as such in the Sources list and should be re-checked against primary or academic works before shipping. Lines marked UNVERIFIED are from general knowledge or a single weak source.

## Timeline

- 1 March 1971: General Yahya Khan postpones the National Assembly session. [3]
- 7 March 1971: Sheikh Mujibur Rahman gives the speech that includes the line "the struggle this time is the struggle for independence". [3][1]
- 25 to 26 March 1971: Pakistan launches Operation Searchlight. Troops moved out from about 11:30 pm on 25 March; Mujibur Rahman was arrested (timeline source gives 1:15 am on 26 March). [3][6]
- 26 March 1971: independence is declared (the date Bangladesh observes as Independence Day). [1][3]
- 27 March 1971: Major Ziaur Rahman broadcasts a declaration from the Swadhin Bangla Betar Kendra radio station at Kalurghat, Chittagong, on behalf of Mujibur Rahman. [3][13]
- Late March to mid April 1971 (resistance phase): Bengali East Bengal Regiment, East Pakistan Rifles, police and Ansar units fight in Chittagong, Kushtia and elsewhere; most major towns fall to Pakistani forces by mid April. [6]
- 10 April 1971: Proclamation of Independence issued and provisional government formed. [2][1]
- 17 April 1971: the cabinet takes the oath at Baidyanathtala (renamed Mujibnagar), Kushtia district, on the Indian border. [2]
- 4 April 1971: General M. A. G. Osmani is named Commander-in-Chief of the Bangladesh forces (per one source; other sources give 11 July for his formal appointment, see Contested points). [4][8]
- May 1971: India opens training centres; Indian Army support to the Mukti Bahini begins (timeline source: 15 May). [3][15]
- 7 July 1971: Z Force, the first regular brigade, is formed. [10]
- 11 to 17 July 1971: first Sector Commanders Conference; Bangladesh Forces organised into 11 sectors, Osmani confirmed as Commander-in-Chief, Lt Col M. A. Rab as Chief of Army Staff. [8][9]
- July to September 1971 (monsoon): the Pakistan Army temporarily regains momentum while Bengali guerrillas continue raids and sabotage; Z Force fights at Kamalpur (31 July) and Ghashipur (10 September). [1][10]
- 2 August 1971: Razakar force established by ordinance (per one source). [14]
- 15 to 16 August 1971: Operation Jackpot, simultaneous naval commando attacks on Chittagong, Chandpur, Narayanganj and Mongla. [5]
- 1 October 1971: S Force formed. 14 October 1971: K Force active. [11]
- 28 September 1971: Bangladesh Air Force (Kilo Flight) formed at Dimapur. [12]
- 21 November 1971: Bangladesh Armed Forces formally established; Indian and Bangladeshi forces placed under a joint command (Mitro Bahini) per one source. [3][7]
- 20 to 21 November 1971: Battle of Garibpur. 22 to 24 November: first Battle of Hilli. [17][16][3]
- 3 December 1971: Pakistan launches pre-emptive air strikes on Indian airfields (Operation Chengiz Khan); India declares war. Kilo Flight flies its first strikes (3 to 4 December). [1][3][12]
- 6 December 1971: Jessore is the first district town liberated (timeline source). [3]
- 7 to 16 December 1971: Battle of Sylhet, including a heliborne landing on 7 December. [18]
- 9 December 1971: Indian forces cross the Meghna using local boats and Mi-4 helicopters. 12 December: within artillery range of Dhaka. [19]
- 16 December 1971: Lt Gen A. A. K. Niazi signs the Instrument of Surrender in Dhaka; roughly 90,000 to 93,000 Pakistani personnel become prisoners. [19][1][3]
- 12 January 1972: the provisional government dissolves. [2]

### Seasonal rhythm (inference from the dates above, not a single sourced claim)

- March to April: conventional-style fighting by defected units, then loss of towns and retreat across the border. [6]
- May to September (including the monsoon): training in India, guerrilla raids and sabotage; Pakistan Army holds towns and roads, momentum tilts to it during the rains. [1][15]
- August onward: coordinated special operations (Operation Jackpot). [5]
- October to November: regular brigades (S, K, Z) and growing numbers of infiltrated guerrillas; border battles with Indian support. [11][15][16][17]
- December (dry season): open conventional war, collapse in about 13 days. [19]

## Bangladeshi forces

### Overall structure

- The Mukti Bahini is described as two parts: regular forces (Niyomito Bahini) drawn from the army, East Pakistan Rifles and police, and irregular people's forces (Gono Bahini) of civilians. [4]
- At the start, defected Bengali strength in Operation Searchlight sources: about 8,000 East Bengal Regiment troops, about 13,000 East Pakistan Rifles and 30,000 or more armed police and Ansar. [6]
- Total Mukti Bahini strength is given as 175,000 (Wikipedia, war article) and 180,000 (Wikipedia, 1971 war article); another account says about 30,000 regulars and over 100,000 guerrillas by December. [3][19][7]
- India reportedly opened 30 training centres in May 1971, rising to 84 by September; each trained 500 to 2,000 guerrillas; total estimated at 100,000. A second figure: by end of November about 83,000 trained, about 50,000 already sent into East Pakistan. [15]

### Regular forces

- East Bengal Regiment (EBR): five battalions revolted in March 1971; the regiment became the core of the new army. [20]
- Bangladesh Forces: organised at the 11 to 17 July 1971 conference under Osmani (C-in-C); the independence forces were grouped into three brigades from EBR battalions. [20][8]
- Z Force (the "Tura Brigade"): formed 7 July 1971; EBR 1st, 3rd, 8th battalions plus an artillery battery; HQ Teldhala near Tura, Meghalaya. [10]
- S Force: formed 1 October 1971; EBR 2nd and 11th battalions. [11]
- K Force: active 14 October to 16 December 1971; EBR 4th, 9th and 10th battalions. [11]
- Sector troops: each sector combined regular troops and guerrillas; sectors were split into sub-sectors under their own commanders. [8][9]

### Irregular forces

- Gono Bahini / Oniyomito (irregular freedom fighters): civilians, mostly youth, trained in Indian camps and sent back across the border in small groups. Groups of five to ten guerrillas per mission are described. [4][15]
- Mujib Bahini (initially Bangladesh Liberation Force): Awami League and Chhatra League activists; organised by Serajul Alam Khan, Sheikh Fazlul Haque Mani, Tofail Ahmed and Abdur Razzaq with Indian help (Maj Gen Sujan Singh Uban, RAW); about 10,000 members at peak; its exact combat role is contested. [21]
- Other left-wing and party-linked units (Kader Bahini, Hemayet Bahini and others) existed. [4]
- Tactics: ambush and sabotage of railways, ports, power plants and shipping. [4]

### Naval and air

- Operation Jackpot: naval commandos trained at the secret C2P camp at Plassey, West Bengal (opened 23 May 1971), by Indian Navy personnel (limpet mines, swimming, survival). 515 trained overall; about 160 took part in the 15 to 16 August attacks. [5]
- Jackpot results (one source): at least 126 ships, coasters or ferries sunk or damaged August to December; also "at least 65 vessels" by November; losses 8 commandos killed and 34 wounded over the campaign. Figures are from a Wikipedia summary and unverified. [5]
- Sector 10 (naval commandos and special forces) was placed directly under the Commander-in-Chief. [9]
- Kilo Flight: formed 28 September 1971 at Dimapur, Nagaland; 3 aircraft (a DHC-3 Otter with rockets, an Alouette III helicopter, a DC-3 Dakota fitted for bombs); 9 Bengali pilots; first strikes on fuel depots at Chittagong and Narayanganj on 3 to 4 December; about 90 sorties 4 to 16 December. [12]
- Some sources state the Bangladesh Air Force was established under Air Commodore A. K. Khandker (per Wikipedia Mukti Bahini article). [4]

### Mujibnagar (provisional) government

- Proclamation of Independence 10 April 1971 served as the interim constitution; oath 17 April at Mujibnagar. [2]
- President: Sheikh Mujibur Rahman (imprisoned in Pakistan). Acting President and Vice President: Syed Nazrul Islam. Prime Minister: Tajuddin Ahmad, also holding defence, information, planning and other portfolios. [2][22]
- Other ministers: foreign affairs, law and parliamentary affairs (Khondakar Mostaq Ahmad); finance, industry and commerce (M. Mansur Ali); home, supply, relief, rehabilitation and agriculture (A. H. M. Qamaruzzaman). [22]
- Government in exile at Calcutta (Kolkata); it set up 11 zonal administrative councils along the border with health, education, relief, police, information and other officers, plus a planning commission and autonomous bodies for youth and refugee welfare. [2]
- Commander-in-Chief: Col (retd) M. A. G. Osmani; Chief of Army Staff: Lt Col M. A. Rab. [8]
- The government coordinated the Mukti Bahini and the Bangladesh armed forces. [22]
- Z Force set up the first civilian administration in liberated Roumari on 27 August 1971. [10]

## Opposing forces

- Pakistan Eastern Command, HQ Dhaka, under Lt Gen A. A. K. Niazi; military adviser Maj Gen Rao Farman Ali; chief of staff Brig Baqir Siddiqui. [23]
- At the start of Operation Searchlight the main formation was the 14th Infantry Division (about 18,000 troops) with an armoured regiment (75 M24 Chaffee tanks), later reinforced by the 9th and 16th Divisions. [6]
- December 1971 order of battle (Wikipedia): 36th ad hoc Division (Dhaka, Tangail, Mymensingh); 14th Division (Sylhet, northern Comilla; HQ Brahmanbaria); 39th ad hoc Division (Comilla, Feni, northern Chittagong; HQ Chandpur); 16th Division (Rajshahi, Bogra, Dinajpur, Rangpur, Pabna); 9th Division (Khulna, Jessore, Kushtia, Faridpur, Barisal, Patuakhali; HQ Jessore). [23]
- Another summary: the east had three divisions plus two ad hoc divisions raised without a real increase in strength; only 5 of 8 promised infantry battalions arrived. [24]
- Paramilitary: East Pakistan Civil Armed Forces in 16 wings across six sectors; Razakars, Mujahids and local militia integrated into divisional areas. [23]
- Strength claims: about 91,000 regulars plus 280,000 paramilitary and 25,000 militia (Wikipedia, war article); 350,000 to 365,000 total with 35,000 Razakars (Wikipedia, 1971 war article). The figures disagree; see Contested points. [3][19]
- Navy: 4 gunboats, 1 patrol boat, 17 armed boats and 24 improvised gunboats; Air Force: one squadron with 20 F-86 Sabres and 3 T-33 trainers. [23]
- Razakars: raised in 1971 as a paramilitary force; one source gives about 50,000 members and an ordinance of 2 August 1971; their stated role was to detain pro-independence Bengalis; they took part in massacres. About 36,000 suspected Razakars were held after the war; Bangladesh's International Crimes Tribunal began in 2010. [14]
- Al-Badr and Al-Shams: locally recruited militias, formed around September 1971 per one source, linked to the Jamaat-e-Islami student wing and to Biharis; used for policing cities and political killings; dissolved 16 December 1971. [25]
- Supply lines and weaknesses: Eastern Command was isolated by distance from West Pakistan, short of supplies, its air force was destroyed early, and the Indian Navy blockaded sea resupply. [19]
- The paramilitary and new militias could not hold out alone against Mukti Bahini attacks with Indian support. [24]
- Pakistan planned to use militias for city policing and regular units for the Indian border. [25]

## Allied support

- Refugees: about 10 million people fled to India (Wikipedia genocide article); more than 60% were Hindus in one estimate, 80 to 90% in another. [26]
- Phases of Indian support: refugee camps and shelter (from March to April); Calcutta HQ and training camps in Assam, Nagaland, West Bengal, Meghalaya (from May); arms, training, intelligence and sector bases for the Mukti Bahini; direct military entry from 3 December. [3][4][15]
- Pre-war border fighting: Battle of Garibpur (20 to 21 November) and Hilli (22 November onward) took place before the formal declaration of war. [17][16]
- Joint Command (Mitro Bahini, "allied forces"): formed 21 November 1971 per Wikipedia, under Lt Gen Jagjit Singh Aurora (Indian Eastern Command); corps: II, XXXIII, IV and 101 Communication Zone; chief of staff Maj Gen J. F. R. Jacob. Other accounts date the formal Joint Command to 4 December; UNVERIFIED which is right. [7]
- Indian ground forces: nine mountain divisions in the plan; one account gives 250,000 troops after 3 December. [19][3]
- Superpower context (two sentences): Cold War alignments shaped the war: the Soviet Union backed India (the Indo-Soviet Treaty of Friendship and Cooperation was signed in August 1971), while the United States leaned toward Pakistan and sent Task Force 74 led by the carrier USS Enterprise toward the Indian Ocean at the height of the war. China supported Pakistan diplomatically. [27][3]

## Key operations

Each line is one event; casualty details are omitted unless a source gave them.

- Chittagong and Kalurghat (late March to early April 1971): Bengali units held positions at Kalurghat Bridge and Railway Hill until Pakistani reinforcements arrived; radio station at Kalurghat used for the 27 March broadcast. [6][13]
- Kushtia (28 to 31 March 1971): local Bengali forces attacked the Pakistani company on 31 March; a counterattack from Jessore ran into a booby trap at Bishakali; a small Pakistani force was overrun. Numbers are from a Wikipedia summary. [28]
- Dhaka (25 to 26 March): Pilkhana EPR HQ (about 2,500 troops) and Rajarbagh police lines resisted and were overwhelmed overnight. [6]
- Kamalpur (31 July) and Ghashipur (10 September): Z Force battles in the north. [10]
- Operation Jackpot (15 to 16 August): see Bangladeshi forces. [5]
- Garibpur (20 to 21 November): Indian infantry and 14 PT-76 tanks defeated a Pakistani counterattack with M24 tanks; later aerial combat followed. [17]
- Hilli (22 to 24 November and 10 to 11 December): fortified position; heavy casualties; Pakistani forces withdrew on 11 December and Indian and Bengali forces advanced toward Bogra. [16]
- Jessore (6 December): first district liberated; Jessore is the HQ of the Pakistani 9th Division. [3][23]
- Sylhet (7 to 16 December): heliborne landing by Mi-4 helicopters on 7 December; later 107 officers, 219 JCOs and 6,190 soldiers in Sylhet surrendered. [18]
- Meghna crossing (9 December): boats and Mi-4 helicopters bypassed destroyed bridges. [19]
- Dhaka approach (12 to 16 December): artillery range by 12 December; ultimatum, then surrender on 16 December. [19]
- Belonia (Dec 1971): UNVERIFIED. The Wikipedia page was not found; no source confirmed details.
- Chittagong naval and port fighting in December: UNVERIFIED beyond the Kilo Flight raid on fuel depots. [12]

## Glossary

Meanings are standard Bengali usage. Items marked (U) were not confirmed against a fetched source and come from general knowledge.

- Mukti Bahini: "Liberation Force"; umbrella name for the Bangladeshi fighting forces. [4]
- Niyomito Bahini: "regular force"; the army, EPR and police defectors. [4]
- Gono Bahini: "people's force"; civilian irregular fighters. [4]
- Oniyomito: "irregular". (U)
- Mukti Fauj: "Liberation Army", an early name for the freedom fighters trained by India. [15]
- Mitro Bahini: "allied forces"; the joint Indian and Bangladeshi command. [7]
- Mujib Bahini: force named for Mujibur Rahman, organised by Awami League and student activists. [21]
- Sector: one of 11 military zones of the war. [8]
- Joy Bangla: "Victory to Bengal"; the war cry and slogan. (U)
- Bangabandhu: "Friend of Bengal"; title of Sheikh Mujibur Rahman. (U)
- Muktijoddha: "freedom fighter". (U)
- Razakar: "volunteer"; the name of the pro-Pakistan paramilitary force. [14]
- Al-Badr, Al-Shams: names of pro-Pakistan militias. [25]
- Mujibnagar: the village where the government took its oath; the name of the government in exile. [2]
- Swadhin Bangla Betar Kendra: "Free Bengal Radio Centre". [13]
- Shaheed: "martyr". (U)
- Bir Uttom, Bir Bikrom: gallantry decorations, for example awarded to Z Force members. [10]
- Bangladesh: "country of Bengal". (U)
- Joydebpur, Pilkhana, Kalurghat: places named in the sources (barracks, EPR headquarters, radio site). [6][13]

## Contested or uncertain points

- Civilian death toll: Bangladesh government 3 million; R. J. Rummel about 1.5 million; independent researchers 300,000 to 500,000; BMJ-linked 2008 study 125,000 to 505,000; US government mid-war estimate 200,000; Hamoodur Rahman Commission (Pakistan) 26,000; Sarmila Bose 50,000 to 100,000; Christian Gerlach (2018) a little over 500,000, not above 1 million. Games should avoid a single number. [26]
- Pakistani military losses and Indian losses (Wikipedia): Pakistan 5,866 killed; India 1,426 to 1,525 killed; Bangladesh about 30,000 killed. Treat as unverified. [3]
- Pakistan Eastern Command size: 91,000 regulars (plus paramilitary) vs 350,000 to 365,000 total; many sources count only about 34,000 to 45,000 combat troops (UNVERIFIED here). [3][19]
- Mukti Bahini size: 175,000 vs 180,000 vs "30,000 regulars + 100,000 guerrillas". [3][19][7]
- Prisoners: 90,000 to 93,000 (varies by whether civilians and paramilitary are counted). [1][3][19]
- Commander-in-Chief date: 4 April (one source) vs 11 July (conference). [4][8]
- Joint Command date: 21 November vs 4 December. [7]
- Independence declaration date: 26 March (official date) vs 27 March (radio broadcast by Maj Zia). [1][13]
- Operation Jackpot totals of ships sunk vary between "65 by November" and "126 to December". [5]
- Role of the Mujib Bahini in combat is debated among historians. [21]
- The refugee religious split (60% to 90% Hindu) varies by estimate. [26]
- "Theria Ghat" as conference site, and details of Belonia and Chittagong December battles: not verified.
- Wikipedia is a tertiary source; replace key figures with Banglapedia, Library of Congress, FRUS Vol. XI or academic sources before final use.
