# Bangladesh 1971: Geography, Logistics and Civilian Context

Research note for the Bangladesh Liberation War strategy-game theme (Bangladeshi perspective). Every fact line ends with a source number from the Sources section. Lines marked RECOMMENDATION are the author's design opinion, not fact. Nothing here is invented; where a point could not be verified it says so.

Quality caveat: many sources below are Wikipedia pages (tertiary) or news features. They were fetched and summarised by a tool, not read in full. Before anything is printed in the game, check key numbers against a primary or academic source (Banglapedia, the Liberation War Museum, FRUS, Hamoodur Rahman Commission, Sarmila Bose, Christian Gerlach). Several preferred sources (Banglapedia, Library of Congress country study, UNHCR archive pages) could not be read in this session (Banglapedia pages returned empty content), so they are not cited.

## 1. Geography

### 1.1 Delta, rivers, climate
- Bangladesh is described as a densely populated, low-lying, mainly riverine country; delta plains of three major river systems cover about 79% of the territory and steep hill ranges in the southeast about 12%. [5]
- Main river systems: Jamuna-Brahmaputra (292 km in Bangladesh), Padma-Ganges (258 km segment), Surma-Meghna (669 km, the longest), and the Karnaphuli near Chittagong. [5]
- About 80% of annual rain falls in the monsoon (June to September); cyclones, floods and tidal bores recur; most land is below 10 m. [5]
- One account says two-thirds of the country lies in the Ganges Delta, that there are more waterways than roads, and that peasant fighters used detailed knowledge of waterways for ambushes and for moving arms. (Opinion essay; author is not a historian.) [14]
- Another account says the 11 war sectors were defined by river systems (Padma, Brahmaputra, Meghna, Jamuna, Surma, Karnaphuli) and that over 300 guerrilla operations used rivers. (News feature; the "300" figure is not independently verified.) [12]
- The Indian Eastern Command's May 1971 draft plan divided Bangladesh into four sectors along the Padma, Jamuna and Meghna rivers. [16]

### 1.2 Wetlands, hills, mangroves
- Haors (bowl-shaped seasonal wetlands) lie in the northeast (Sunamganj, Habiganj, Maulvibazar, Sylhet, with extensions to Kishoreganj and Netrokona), bounded by the Meghalaya hills to the north and Tripura/Mizoram to the south; about 47 major haors and 6,300 beels; flooded roughly July to November, turning mound settlements into islands. [22]
- Sylhet hills: not separately verified in this session (the haor source places the haor basin against Meghalaya and Tripura/Mizoram hills). [22]
- Chittagong Hill Tracts: about 13,295 km2 in the southeast, bordering Tripura and Mizoram (India) and Myanmar; steep slopes, narrow valleys, evergreen forest; highest point Keokradong 986 m; the Karnaphuli flows through to the Bay of Bengal. [21]
- Sundarbans: world's largest mangrove forest, about 10,277 km2 in total, about 60% in Khulna Division (about 6,017 km2 in Bangladesh) and 40% in West Bengal; a net of tidal waterways, mudflats and islands; near Mongla and Khulna. [20]

### 1.3 Border with India
- The refugees mainly sheltered in the bordering Indian states of West Bengal, Assam, Meghalaya and Tripura. [7]
- Training camps for the Bengali forces were run in Indian states including Bihar, Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram, Meghalaya, Tripura and West Bengal (Wikipedia list; Arunachal Pradesh and Nagaland look unusual and were not cross-checked). [2]
- Mukti Bahini were allowed by India to cross the border at will (as stated by one encyclopedia entry). [2]

### 1.4 Towns, ports, transport
- Chittagong port sits on the Karnaphuli and handles over 70% of Bangladesh's export-import trade today. [27]
- Mongla port (founded 1950 as Chalna, moved 1954) is at the Pasur and Mongla river confluence about 48 km south of Khulna, about 100 km from the Bay of Bengal, among the Sundarbans; today the second seaport. [26]
- Hardinge Bridge: steel railway truss bridge over the Padma (Ishwardi/Pabna to Bheramara/Kushtia), completed 1912; severely damaged on 13 December 1971 by Indian Air Force bombing while the Pakistani army retreated toward Jessore. [30]
- Bhairab railway bridge over the Meghna (Ashuganj to Bhairab Bazar), opened 6 December 1937, link between Dhaka and Chittagong rail traffic. (Search summary of the Tongi-Bhairab-Akhaura line page; not read in full.) [34]
- Poongli Bridge over the Lohajang river in Tangail was the aim of the 11-12 December 1971 Tangail airdrop, intended to block the retreat of Pakistan's 93rd Brigade toward Dhaka and open a road to Dhaka. [28]
- The Meghna River crossing (9-12 December 1971) and the Meghna Heli Bridge are named as significant river-crossing actions. [23][1] I could not read a dedicated page for the Heli Bridge, so details are unverified.
- Daily Star feature says a critical rail bridge near Feni linking Dhaka and Chittagong was among bridges destroyed. [15]

### 1.5 Monsoon, and guerrilla versus conventional forces
- The Pakistan Army is described as regaining momentum during the monsoon while Bengali fighters also mounted counteroffensives. [1]
- The Bangladesh monsoon (April to October, per one source) is said to create about 300 additional navigable channels, making waterway control critical to Pakistani logistics. [10]
- Pakistani doctrine held "the defence of the east lies in the west": forces in the east were to hold territory until victory in the western theatre. [16]
- In September-October 1971 General Niazi adopted a "fortress concept", holding key cities (Jessore, Bogra, Rangpur, Comilla, Chittagong) with fallback lines. [16]
- Indian planners (August 1971) assumed isolated Pakistani forces in the "Dhaka Bowl" would capitulate once seaports fell; the Indian draft plan stressed bypassing fortified positions and taking communication hubs. [16]
- The Mukti Bahini used groups of five to ten guerrillas on specific missions, hitting bridges, culverts, fuel depots and ships to reduce Pakistani mobility. [2]
- US Consul General Dacca, 20 October 1971: the insurgency could not challenge Pakistani forces in cities but ran effective ambushes and hit-and-run attacks in rural areas, especially Dacca, Comilla and Noakhali districts; coordination and communication problems persisted; the insurgents showed ship mining and effective bridge sabotage. [13]

## 2. Logistics

### 2.1 Liberation forces
- Training: Operation Jackpot (Indian Army-run training and supply operation, from 15 May 1971 per one search summary) trained fighters for four to six weeks in West Bengal, Bihar, Assam, Tripura and Meghalaya. (Search summary; unverified at page level.) [33]
- Strength: one source gives about 70,000 Mukti Bahini by end of November, plus about 50,000 irregulars trained by India; another says more than 70,000 soldiers, guerrillas and sailors. Figures differ in their wording and are approximate. [33][15]
- Eleven sectors under defector officers; first sector commanders' conference 11-17 July 1971 (one source says 10-17 July). [2][6]
- Weapons: early arms came from Pakistani depots seized in March-April 1971; further equipment was bought through the Calcutta arms market (the source's list includes howitzers and aircraft; treat the exact list with caution). [2]
- Indian support: authorised in April 1971 by Prime Minister Indira Gandhi (diplomatic, economic and military), including training and use of bases. [2]
- Local support, food and money flows to sectors, medical services: I could not find a reliable source in this session. Waterways were used to carry arms and ammunition (opinion essay, see 1.1). [14] The role of villagers and sector medical arrangements remain UNVERIFIED here and need a primary source (for example Banglapedia entries or sector commanders' memoirs).
- Naval commandos: Operation Jackpot (naval part) 25 April to 15 August 1971, main attacks 16 August; secret camp C2P at Plassey, West Bengal, from 23 May; 300 volunteers initially, 499 trained eventually; eight Bengali naval personnel and 20 Indian instructors; targets Chittagong, Chandpur, Narayanganj, Mongla. [10] (A different page gives 15 August as the date. [2])
- Reported results: at least 65 vessels sunk between August and November (about 100,000 tons of shipping sunk or crippled) [10]; another account says 126 ships, coasters and ferries sunk or damaged August-December, and names Operation Hot Pants (two patrol craft commissioned 30 October; Mongla entrance mined 10 November). [12] Counts differ by source.
- Sabotage totals: 231 bridges, 122 railway lines and 90 power installations damaged or destroyed, April-November 1971, attributed to Siddiq Salik's memoir Witness to Surrender (a Pakistani officer) and repeated in a 2020 Daily Star essay. [15] The same essay claims 237 officers and 3,695 soldiers killed or wounded by November; this is not independently verified. [15]
- Communications and morale: Swadhin Bangla Betar Kendra began from Kalurghat, Chittagong, on 26-27 March 1971; after air attack on 30 March it moved to Agartala, Tripura (3 April); ran in Bengali, English and Urdu until 6 December 1971; broadcasters included Kamal Lohani and M. R. Akhtar Mukul; the 27 March declaration read by Major Ziaur Rahman was picked up by a ship and retransmitted by Radio Australia and the BBC. [4]
- Mujibnagar government: proclaimed 10 April 1971; cabinet oath 17 April at Baidyanathtala (Mujibnagar), Meherpur/Kushtia; operated from Calcutta (9 Circus Avenue, later 8 Theatre Road); President Sheikh Mujibur Rahman (imprisoned), Acting President Syed Nazrul Islam, Prime Minister Tajuddin Ahmad, Foreign Minister Khondaker Mostaq Ahmad; military command under Colonel M. A. G. Osmani; missions in New Delhi, Washington and London; dissolved 12 January 1972. [3]
- Diplomacy: Pakistan's deputy high commissioner in Calcutta, M. Hossain Ali, and Bengali staff switched allegiance; Justice Abu Sayeed Chowdhury in London was chief overseas representative; diplomatic aims were to publicise the genocide, win humanitarian aid for refugees and keep close contact with sympathetic states. (News summary.) [32]
- Funding: no figures found for the exile government's budget. UNVERIFIED.
- Strategy disagreement: in July 1971 Osmani favoured sending many guerrillas inside Bangladesh to hold areas and seek recognition, while Indian planners preferred a smaller elite force. [16]

### 2.2 Pakistani logistics
- East and West Pakistan were separated by over 1,500 km of Indian territory. After the January 1971 Ganga hijacking India banned Pakistani overflights, forcing aircraft to detour via Ceylon, over 4,600 km longer. (Search summary of several articles; details unverified at page level.) [31]
- Reinforcements were flown from West Pakistan by C-130 and commercial PIA flights. [11]
- Military cantonments' cargo needs were put at two lakh tons and 1.5 lakh personnel, said to double by mid-1971 (single search summary; unverified). [31]
- Forces: the 14th Infantry Division (about 18,000 plus troops, four brigades, one armoured regiment of 75 M24 Chaffee tanks) at the start; Bengali defectors about 8,000 regulars, 13,000 paramilitary, 30,000 plus police and volunteers. [11]
- Ground control: Pakistan relied on cities and the road, rail and river net; sabotage of bridges, ports and power was meant to slow convoys. [2][15] Frogmen focused on seaports and the river ports that were the "main arteries of communication". (Search summary.) [31]
- Late-war: Indian Navy blockade of East Pakistan, airfield at Dacca put out of commission by the Indian Air Force. [23]
- Surrender: 16 December 1971 at Ramna Race Course, Dacca, signed by Lt Gen A. A. K. Niazi and Lt Gen J. S. Aurora; about 93,000 Pakistani personnel captured (the military/civilian split is disputed, see section 6). [29][23]

## 3. Civilian context

Brief and non-graphic by design. The war began for civilians with Operation Searchlight on 25-26 March 1971; the main phase is dated to 20 May 1971. [11]

### 3.1 Refugees and displacement
- About 10 million people fled to India; up to 30 million were internally displaced (from a population of about 70 million). Wikipedia notes that 80-90% of the refugees were Hindus. [8][1]
- Inflow reached about 100,000 a day by May 1971 and one million refugees were in India by May. [7]
- One news account gives about 825 camps holding about 6.8 million people, with about 3 million staying with host families, and 492 camps in West Bengal alone. (Single source; not cross-checked.) [7]
- Oxfam managed relief for about 600,000 refugees across camps in West Bengal, Assam, Meghalaya and Tripura, kept a warehouse in Calcutta, and published "The Testimony of Sixty" in October 1971. [17]
- Relief pledges began at about $17 million and reached about $70 million by June (UNHCR-led fundraising, per one news account). [7]
- Repatriation: large returns January-March 1972; about 60,000 still in India by March 1972. [7]
- UNHCR first took on large-scale relief work through this crisis. [UNHCR own archive not read; search summary only] (Treat as unverified.)

### 3.2 How the toll of killed is stated (contested)
Figures by source, as listed on Wikipedia: Bangladeshi government 3 million; Hamoodur Rahman Commission (Pakistan) 26,000; Sarmila Bose 50,000-100,000; R. J. Rummel about 1.5 million; Christian Gerlach (2018) slightly over 500,000; British Medical Journal study (2008) 125,000-505,000; independent researchers 300,000-500,000; a mid-war CIA/State Department figure of 200,000. [8] The main war article gives a general civilian range of 300,000-3,000,000. [1] The 3 million figure is described as embedded in Bangladeshi culture and disputed by some scholars. [8] I did not verify these against the original publications.
- Terminology: "genocide" is used in major Bangladeshi publications and was used by US diplomats in the "Blood Telegram"; at the time most UN members rejected such allegations, and the 1971 events appear rarely in genocide-studies textbooks. [8]
- Estimates of sexual violence (200,000-400,000) are challenged by some scholars. [8] (Stated only as a contested range; the game should not depict it.)
- Combatant figures also vary: about 30,000 Mukti Bahini killed; India 1,426-1,525 killed; Pakistan 5,866 killed and 90,000-93,000 captured. [1]

### 3.3 Remembrance and museums
- The Liberation War Museum (Dhaka) was founded 22 March 1996 as a citizens' initiative with crowd-funded donations; its new building opened 16 April 2017; about 21,000 objects. [9][19]
- Its galleries cover early history, the Bengali Language Movement, the March 1971 non-cooperation movement, the genocide and resistance, the declaration of independence, refugees and the Mukti Bahini. [9][18]
- Its stated mission: dedicated to freedom-loving people and to victims of atrocities "committed in the name of religion, ethnicity and sovereignty", linking history to current human rights issues, remembrance across religious and ethnic lines. [18][19]
- It runs school programs and travelling exhibits across 64 districts; describes the war as the worst genocide since the Second World War (its own framing); exhibits excavated remains from killing fields. [19]
- 14 December is observed in Bangladesh as the Day of the Martyred Intellectuals. [8]

## 4. Sensitive-treatment guidance

### 4.1 What respected sources emphasise (findings)
- Remembrance and dignity of victims, across religious and ethnic lines. [18]
- Language-movement roots and the civil non-cooperation movement before the shooting war. [9]
- The sacrifice of both civilians and freedom fighters, and the refugee experience (10 million), alongside the guerrilla story. [19][9]
- Teaching the young to oppose atrocity everywhere, not only to commemorate one side. [18]
- International humanitarian response (Oxfam, UNHCR, Indian public) as part of the story. [17][7]
- Honest uncertainty: figures are contested, so museums and scholars state sources. [8]

### 4.2 RECOMMENDATIONS (author's opinion, not fact)
Do:
1. Frame the player as leading a liberation movement and civil government in exile, with the aim of ending the violence and winning independence; make victory about restoring safety and return of refugees.
2. Show civilians as people with needs and voices (villagers who shelter, families in camps, doctors, radio staff). Offer "protect" and "relieve" objectives, not "harvest".
3. Open with a short remembrance note and a source list; put contested numbers in an in-game encyclopedia with ranges and who claims what.
4. Name real institutions and dates correctly (Mujibnagar, Swadhin Bangla Betar, the 11 sectors) and consult Bangladeshi historians and the museum before release.
5. Make Indian support, international relief, and the radio and diplomacy front visible as real contributors to the outcome.
6. Give the opposing side human, non-cartoon units; separate individual soldiers from command decisions; Pakistani civilians and Bengali minorities should not be shown as enemies.
7. Include the language movement and the March 1971 non-cooperation movement as the story's opening chapters.
8. Offer a content setting (and avoid explicit depiction), with a plain "learn more" link to the museum and Banglapedia.

Don't:
1. Do not treat civilians, refugees or the dead as a resource, score, currency or collectible; avoid a "refugees" commodity entirely (use food, shelter, medicine, labour as separate relief goods under protection).
2. Do not use atrocity, massacre or sexual violence as a mechanic, event card, achievement or score.
3. Do not let civilian deaths be an acceptable "cost of winning" in the scoring system; no sacrifice-for-bonus loops.
4. Do not caricature either side (no cowardly, brutal or comic ethnic or religious stereotypes).
5. Do not present the 3 million figure, or the lower ones, as settled fact; present the range.
6. Do not enter the Hindu-Muslim framing carelessly: 80-90% of refugees are reported as Hindu [8], but the movement was a linguistic and political one with a secular outlook (as the museum states [19]); avoid reducing it to a religious war.
7. Do not trivialise with jokes, loot boxes, monetisation or a "fun" tone around camps and killing fields; do not use real victims' faces or photographs.
8. Do not invent real people as characters in dishonourable or unrealistic roles; use composite fictional characters, with real leaders only in respectful, accurate cameo.

## 6. Contested or uncertain points

- Civilian death toll: 26,000 to 3,000,000 depending on source (section 3.2). [8][1]
- Refugee total: "about 10 million" is the usual figure; camp counts, host-family counts and religious breakdown come from single sources. [7][8]
- Prisoners of war: one page says about 93,000, with 79,676 to 81,000 uniformed plus 10,324-12,500 civilians [23]; another says only 34,000-45,000 were military personnel [29]. Unreconciled.
- Sector tables disagree: a Wikipedia table lists HQs as Sector 4 Karimganj/Masimpur, 5 Banshtola, 6 Burimari, 8 Benapole, 9 Hasnabad [6]; a separate search summary (page not identified) listed Sector 4 Khoai, 5 Shillong, 6 Patgram, 8 Kalayani, 9 Tarangapur. Commander dates also differ (Sector 1: Zia until May vs June). Use sector areas, not HQ names, until checked against Banglapedia. [6]
- Sector 10: a Wikipedia table calls it the Bay of Bengal naval command [6]; the other summary says it was directly under the commander-in-chief with naval commandos. Consistent in substance.
- Sector 11: commanders vary by month in sources. [6]
- Sabotage totals (231 / 122 / 90) come originally from one Pakistani officer's memoir via an essay; verified only as a repeated claim. [15]
- Number of ships sunk: 65 vs 126 depending on counting window. [10][12]
- Mukti Bahini strength: 70,000 plus irregulars; other figures exist. [33][15]
- Operation Jackpot: the name is used both for the Indian-run training and supply operation from 15 May and for the naval commando raid; dates vary (15 or 16 August). [33][10][2]
- Terrain claims like "Pakistanis terrified of water" come from popular essays, not research. Excluded.
- Not verified at all: funding of the exile government, village support networks, medical services in sectors, ferry names, haor operations in detail, any statistics on trained guerrillas by camp, rail/road capacity numbers.
- The Wikipedia main article lists Mukti Bahini dead (~30,000) alongside very uncertain civilian dead; do not present as precise. [1]
- No instructions addressed to the assistant were found in any fetched page.
