## How to read this, and the limits of the research

- **Banglapedia could not be read directly.** Every Banglapedia URL tried (`/index.php/...`, `?title=...`, with and without commas) returned only the text "Hello World :-)" through the fetch tool, and the web archive is blocked for the tool. Banglapedia statements below come from **search-result summaries of its pages** (sources 1, 2). They are usable as pointers but are not verbatim page reads. They are marked "(Banglapedia, via search summary)".
- Pakistani army-history PDFs (aimh.gov.pk) failed a certificate check; the Hamoodur Rahman Commission report, the Library of Congress country study text, FRUS XI and the Liberation War Museum site were not read in full. They are listed under "unverified".
- Several "independent" sources are Wikipedia-derived or cite the same Indian and Bangladeshi histories. Where two sources agree only because one copies the other, the verdict says so.
- No page contained instructions addressed to the researcher.
- Verdicts: VERIFIED = two or more sources agree; PARTLY = broadly right but needs a correction or sources conflict; CONTRADICTED = better sources say otherwise; NOT FOUND = no usable support located.

## Item 1. The 11 sector areas and sub-sector counts

Two independent-ish lists were compared: the Daily Star lists (sources 3, 4; both derive from Bangladesh Army and Liberation War histories) and the Wikipedia list (source 5). Sub-sector counts agree for sectors 1, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9 and 11, and **disagree for sectors 2, 5 and 6**. Headquarters disagree or are missing for several sectors.

**Count-of-sectors framing:** all sources agree there were 11 sectors, divided into sub-sectors, and that the organisation was set at the July 1971 sector commanders' conference (see item 2). Banglapedia (via search summary) says the whole of Bangladesh "would be divided into 11 sectors".

## Item 2. Dates

| Claim | Verdict | Corrected / best-supported statement | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| C-in-C Osmani appointed **4 April 1971** | CONTRADICTED as an appointment date | The 4 April date appears in one Wikipedia sentence ("established the Bangladesh Armed Forces on 4 April"). Better-supported sequence: he was **elected commander of the Bengali forces at the Teliapara meeting on 9 April**; his Wikipedia infobox gives **12 April** as the start of command; he was **appointed/took the oath as Commander-in-Chief with the government on 17 April 1971**. Banglapedia (via search summary) matches: assumed command 12 April, C-in-C 17 April. | 8, 9, 1 |
| C-in-C appointment **11 July 1971** | CONTRADICTED as the first appointment; right as a date of confirmation/structure | 11 to 17 July 1971 was the first **sector commanders' conference** (Banglapedia, via search summary, places it at Calcutta/Mujibnagar; another source says Mujibnagar, 12 to 17 July, chaired by the Prime Minister). It set the 11-sector structure and the force organisation. One compilation says the "interim provincial government of July 11" appointed him; treat that as the conference, not a first appointment. | 1, 9, 16, 6 |
| Joint Command **21 November** vs **4 December** | PARTLY (both dates have real support; not resolved) | **21 November 1971**: Bangladesh's official Armed Forces Day, when the army, navy and air force were "fully operational and launched a coordinated offensive" (afd.gov.bd); Wikipedia and one Daily Star article also say the Indian and Bangladeshi forces were put under a joint command (Mitro Bahini) that day. **4 December 1971**: Banglapedia (via search summary) and other Bangladeshi accounts say the **Joint Command of the Bangladesh Liberation Force and the Indian Eastern Command was formed on 4 December**, with a joint-forces agreement signed that day. Safest wording: "joint command in place by early December (21 November is Bangladesh's Armed Forces Day; the formal joint command is dated 4 December in Bangladeshi sources)". | 10, 11, 12, 27, 1 |
| Z Force formed **7 July 1971** | VERIFIED (month); day single-source | Z Force: first regular brigade; formed 7 July 1971 (Wikipedia); Banglapedia (via search summary) says "created in July". Wikipedia adds that Osmani had announced the decision on 13 June. Battalions 1st, 3rd, 8th East Bengal. HQ Teldhala near Tura, Meghalaya ("Tura Brigade"). | 13, 1 |
| S Force formed **1 October 1971** | VERIFIED (month); day single-source | Banglapedia (via search summary): "created in October". Wikipedia: 1 October 1971. Battalions 2nd and 11th East Bengal. | 15, 1 |
| K Force formed **14 October 1971** | PARTLY | Wikipedia: active 14 October to 16 December 1971. Banglapedia (via search summary) gives no day and does not give the date of formation. Battalions 4th, 9th, 10th East Bengal (plus a field artillery battery). Use "October 1971". | 14, 1, 12 |
| Constituent battalions of Z, S, K | VERIFIED | Z = 1, 3, 8 East Bengal; S = 2, 11 East Bengal; K = 4, 9, 10 East Bengal. All sources agree. | 1, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16 |
| Mujibnagar **proclamation 10 April** | VERIFIED | The Proclamation of Independence (provisional constitution) is dated 10 April 1971 and the provisional government is dated from then. | 17, 18, 9 |
| Mujibnagar **oath 17 April** | VERIFIED | The cabinet was sworn in on 17 April 1971 at Baidyanathtala mango grove (renamed Mujibnagar) in Meherpur sub-division of greater Kushtia district; the Proclamation was read out there. | 17, 18 |
| **26 vs 27 March** declaration framing | PARTLY (framing needs care) | 26 March is the official Independence Day. The Daily Star's study of the contemporary press says the world press reported independence mainly from **26 March** broadcasts at Kalurghat (announcing that "the Sheikh has declared" independence), not from Major Zia's later announcements. Major Zia broadcast on **27 March** (evening) in the name of Mujibur Rahman and made further broadcasts on 28 to 30 March. Safest wording: "declaration attributed to Mujibur Rahman in the early hours of 26 March, spread by radio from 26 to 27 March, and read on air by Major Zia on 27 March on his behalf". | 19, 21, 9 |
| **3 December** start of open war | PARTLY (right, but only secondary sources) | Widely stated: Pakistan's pre-emptive air strikes on Indian airfields on 3 December and India's declaration of war that night. Support here is Wikipedia-level and the liberationwar.org timeline; no primary/FRUS page was read. Border fighting (Garibpur, Hilli) and the 21 November Armed Forces Day offensive began earlier, so "open conventional war" starts 3 December only for the Indian-Pakistani state war. | 9, 6, 24 |
| **16 December** surrender | VERIFIED | Surrender in Dhaka on 16 December 1971; Bangladesh's Victory Day. Consistent across Bangladeshi and Indian sources read. | 11, 17, 27 |

Also corrected: 01 lists "Lt Col M. A. Rab as Chief of Army Staff" with Osmani confirmed as C-in-C on 11 to 17 July. Sources agree on the posts (C-in-C, Deputy Chief and Air Chief, Chief of Army Staff) but the appointments belong to the April government and the July reorganisation, not to a single 11 July act.

## Item 3. How the Z, S and K Force names were chosen

| Claim | Verdict | Corrected statement | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Names unknown (our files said "not stated") | VERIFIED: sources do say | Banglapedia (via search summary): Z Force was named after the first letter of its commander's name; S Force after the initial of its commander's name; K Force likewise. Daily Star, Wikipedia and a Daily Star training article say the three brigades were named after their commanders. Wikipedia's S Force page hedges: the designation "likely derives from" the commander's surname. One variant: Z Force is also called the "Tura Brigade" after its base. | 1, 13, 14, 15, 16 |
| Risk L-20 (names tied to real commanders) | CONFIRMED | The letters are initials of real, named commanders, so the risk is real. If the game uses fictional commanders, it should not use "Z/S/K Force" labels as if they were initials of fictional names; use generic brigade names or label them "first, second, third regular brigade". | 1, 13 to 16 |

## Item 4. Roumari civil administration, 27 August 1971

| Claim | Verdict | Corrected statement | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Z Force set up the first civilian administration in liberated Roumari on 27 August 1971 | PARTLY | Wikipedia says Ziaur Rahman "inaugurated the first ever administration system in a liberated area" on 27 August 1971, citing a 1998 book by Shafaat Jamil. A second source gives 28 August (a search snippet from a party-linked biography page; weak). An article summarising Bangladeshi blog research says the build-up was **6 August** (a lieutenant of the 3rd Bengal ordered to hold the area), **8 August** (citizens' committee), **11 August** (visit with an Indian general), and then a hospice, customs office, post office, police station and fighters' training centre. The exact day of the formal administration is 27 or 28 August; "first in a liberated area" is the Z Force account and is not confirmed by an independent neutral source. | 13, 20, 30 |

**Best statement for the game:** "In August 1971, in liberated Roumari, Z Force and local citizens set up a civil committee, a hospital, post office, police station and customs post." Use the month, not the day.

## Item 5. Pakistan Eastern Command in December 1971; fortress towns; tanks

| Claim | Verdict | Corrected statement | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eastern Command HQ Dhaka under Lt Gen Niazi | VERIFIED | Consistent in every source read. | 22, 23, 24 |
| Five divisional formations: 36th ad hoc (Dhaka, Tangail, Mymensingh); 14th (Sylhet, northern Comilla; HQ Brahmanbaria); 39th ad hoc (Comilla, Feni, northern Chittagong; HQ Chandpur); 16th (Rajshahi, Bogra, Dinajpur, Rangpur, Pabna; HQ Bogra); 9th (Khulna, Jessore, Kushtia, Faridpur, Barisal, Patuakhali; HQ Jessore) | VERIFIED | Two independent descriptions agree: three regular divisions (9th, 14th, 16th) and two **ad hoc** divisional headquarters (36th and 39th) set up around the middle of November 1971 with four ad hoc brigade headquarters, without a real increase in troops. Only five infantry battalions arrived of the reinforcements Niazi requested. Areas of 14th and 39th: the Wikipedia orders place 14th in Sylhet/Brahmanbaria and the 39th at Comilla, Noakhali and Chandpur. | 22, 23, 24 |
| "Fortress" towns: Jessore, Bogra, Rangpur, Comilla, Chittagong | PARTLY | The "fortress concept" is verified (border towns on likely Indian axes prepared with 45 days of rations and two months of ammunition). The named fortresses are **Jessore, Jhenida (Jhenaidah), Bogra, Rangpur, Comilla and Bhairab Bazar**, plus **independent defence zones at Chittagong and Sylhet**. Another summary lists Jessore, Jhenaidah, Sylhet, Comilla and Rangpur. Our list missed Jhenida and Bhairab Bazar and put Chittagong in the wrong category (a defence zone). Jessore was the strongest. | 23, 24 |
| 75 M24 Chaffee tanks at the start | NOT FOUND in a strong source | The figure "one armoured regiment with 75 M24 Chaffee tanks" is repeated from Wikipedia's Operation Searchlight page (March 1971, 14th Division). I found no independent confirmation. December detail points to smaller numbers: 29 Cavalry (16th Division) with three squadrons and independent armoured squadrons elsewhere; 3rd Independent Armoured Squadron had 14 Chaffees at Garibpur (destroyed 21 to 22 November; an Indian Defence Review text says 13 Chaffees lost); 4 Chaffees at the Kushtia tank ambush on 9 December. | 25, 22, 24, 28 |
| Navy and air figures (4 gunboats; 20 F-86) | PARTLY | Wikipedia OOB says 20 F-86 and 4 gunboats; the Indian Defence Review source says "about 16" Sabres at Dhaka. Treat as 16 to 20. | 22, 24 |

**Best statement for the game:** "five divisional formations (three regular, two raised in November), strongpoints at border towns (Jessore, Jhenaidah, Bogra, Rangpur, Comilla, Bhairab Bazar) and defence zones at Chittagong and Sylhet; a handful of light-tank squadrons". Do not print a tank total.
